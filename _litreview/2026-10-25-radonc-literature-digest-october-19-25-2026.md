# Weekly Radiation Oncology Literature Surveillance Digest: October 19–25, 2026

## Bottom Line This Week

This is a **clinically important week**, led by a large randomized breast-radiotherapy trial and a practical thoracic reirradiation consensus. The clearest potentially practice-changing result is **HypoMast-IMNI**, a phase III noninferiority trial of 1,494 randomized patients evaluating **42.56 Gy in 16 fractions versus 50 Gy in 25 fractions** after mastectomy, including internal mammary nodal irradiation. Five-year locoregional recurrence was 1.7% versus 2.2%, respectively; the prespecified noninferiority criterion was met. Grade 3 acute skin toxicity was lower with hypofractionation (1.7% versus 12.2%). This reinforces shorter postmastectomy courses even when internal mammary coverage is required, although its single-centre population and limited representation of neoadjuvant-treated patients matter.[^1]

The second high-value publication is a **joint ESTRO lung/reirradiation focus-group and ReCOG consensus** on how to plan and deliver lung reirradiation. It addresses a real clinical safety gap: overlapping high-dose thoracic courses, registration and cumulative-dose uncertainty, motion management, and technically defensible treatment workflows. This is **expert consensus, not a randomized guideline establishing safe universal cumulative constraints**.[^2]

A **new randomized PET-guided dose-escalation study in stage III non-small-cell lung cancer** appeared online October 23. Its question is important after the toxicity and survival lessons of uniform dose escalation: can a metabolically targeted boost improve locoregional control? The publication and randomized design were verified, but the numerical outcome results and DOI could not be independently retrieved in this surveillance pass. It is therefore ranked as a high-priority **read-the-primary-paper item**, not characterized as positive or practice-changing.[^3]

The most interesting AI/workflow publication is a peer-reviewed **agentic BT-RADS glioma follow-up system** integrating note extraction, imaging segmentation, and rule-based response assessment. It agreed with expert scoring in 76.0% of 492 evaluable examinations, versus 57.5% for original clinical assessments. This is a meaningful end-to-end demonstration, but the evidence remains retrospective and single-centre, and a 24% overall disagreement rate precludes unsupervised use.[^4] A radiotherapy-data-standards report is also operationally relevant: data interoperability is foundational to reliable automation, multi-institutional research, and auditability.[^5]

The remaining papers are primarily **early-phase, observational, review, or mechanistic**. Papaverine plus lung SBRT has phase I safety/feasibility data, not evidence of radiosensitization benefit. Thoracic heart-rate changes may offer a future toxicity signal but are not a validated NTCP tool. No new preprint from the searched arXiv, medRxiv, or bioRxiv results cleared the threshold for inclusion over the peer-reviewed work. **Overall importance: high for breast fractionation and thoracic reirradiation; moderate for AI and workflow; low for immediate translation of the remaining findings.**

Publication window: **October 19–25, 2026**, prioritized by first online publication date rather than later journal issue assignment. This is a curated surveillance digest, not a formal systematic review. Where publisher access prevented confirmation of numerical results or bibliographic details, the uncertainty is stated explicitly rather than filled in by inference.

## Must Read

### 1. Hypofractionated Versus Conventionally Fractionated Postmastectomy Radiotherapy With Internal Mammary Node Irradiation for High-Risk Breast Cancer (HypoMast-IMNI): A Randomized Phase III Trial

Citation: Wang X, Zhang X, Chen X, Yang Z, Mei X, Zhang L, Meng J, Shi W, Luo J, Jin K, Gu Y, Mo M, Zhang Z, Yu K, Liu G, Wu J, Shao Z, Guo X, Yu X, Ma J. Hypofractionated Versus Conventionally Fractionated Postmastectomy Radiotherapy With Internal Mammary Node Irradiation for High-Risk Breast Cancer (HypoMast-IMNI): A Randomized Phase III Trial. *Journal of Clinical Oncology*. Published online October 19, 2026.[^1]

Journal/source: Journal of Clinical Oncology  
Publication type: Peer-reviewed randomized phase III noninferiority trial  
One-line takeaway: Sixteen-fraction postmastectomy radiotherapy including internal mammary nodes was noninferior to 25 fractions for locoregional control, with less grade 3 acute skin toxicity.

Evidence tier: High. Randomized phase III trial; 1,494 randomized and 1,429 included in the modified intention-to-treat analysis, with median follow-up of 58.1 months.

Importance score: 9.5/10

Key limitations:

- Single-centre trial; patients treated after neoadjuvant chemotherapy were excluded, limiting application to contemporary neoadjuvant pathways.
- All patients underwent axillary lymph-node dissection, and very-high-risk N3 subgroups were underrepresented.
- Locoregional failures were unexpectedly rare (26 total); noninferiority used a prespecified margin and modified intention-to-treat analysis.
- Five-year follow-up is reassuring but insufficient to exclude later ischemic cardiac or other delayed effects.

Practical radiation oncology relevance:
The schedules were **42.56 Gy/16 fractions** and **50 Gy/25 fractions**, both covering chest wall and regional nodes including the internal mammary chain. Five-year locoregional recurrence was **1.7% versus 2.2%** (HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.32–1.51; noninferiority P = .017), without significant differences in disease-free or overall survival. Grade 3 acute skin reactions were **1.7% versus 12.2%**. Grade ≥2 pneumonitis was approximately 2% in both groups; no ischemic cardiac events were recorded within available follow-up. This is immediately relevant to breast-radiotherapy consultation and departmental fractionation policy, especially where internal mammary coverage has historically made clinicians reluctant to shorten treatment. It supports a specific 16-fraction regimen, not unrestricted extrapolation to every five-fraction nodal schedule.

### 2. How to Plan and Deliver Lung Cancer Reirradiation: A Consensus Approach From the ESTRO Reirradiation and Lung Focus Groups and ReCOG

Citation: Hoffmann L, Matrosic CK, Andratschke N, Appelt A, Beddok A, Bergman A, Duffton A, Dunne EM, Gan GN, Mayo C, Murrell DH, O'Dwyer N, Palma D, Velec M, Willmann J, Yorke E, Popovic M, Simone CB II. How to plan and deliver lung cancer reirradiation: A consensus approach from the ESTRO reirradiation and lung focus groups and ReCOG. *Radiotherapy and Oncology*. Published online October 21, 2026; article 111837.[^2]

Journal/source: Radiotherapy and Oncology  
Publication type: Peer-reviewed multidisciplinary expert consensus / technical practice guidance  
One-line takeaway: ESTRO and ReCOG outline a reproducible, risk-aware technical framework for evaluating and delivering high-dose thoracic reirradiation.

Evidence tier: Expert consensus informed by heterogeneous clinical and technical literature; not randomized comparative evidence.

Importance score: 9/10

Key limitations:

- Lung reirradiation remains supported mainly by retrospective series and heterogeneous institutional experience.
- The paper is a consensus approach rather than a prospectively validated set of universal safe cumulative dose limits.
- Uncertainty in prior-plan recovery, deformable registration, EQD2 conversion, and changing anatomy can materially affect cumulative-dose estimates.

Practical radiation oncology relevance:
This is a **keep-at-hand** paper for recurrent or second-primary thoracic cancers after previous radiation. The essential clinical problem is not simply whether a new plan meets current organ-at-risk constraints; it is whether the **combined** treatment burden, spatial overlap, interval, motion, and organ vulnerability are acceptable. A standardized workflow should explicitly address retrieval and review of prior plans, image registration, dose summation and its uncertainty, respiratory motion, target delineation, peer review, and patient counselling. Because the full technical tables were not independently extracted in this surveillance pass, no numerical cumulative-dose limits are reproduced here. The consensus is particularly valuable for SBRT and central/ultracentral retreatment, where rare catastrophic toxicity is a major concern.

### 3. PET-Guided Dose Escalation for Stage III Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer: A Randomized Clinical Trial

Citation: Raman S, Bissonnette JP, Palma DA, Warner A, Wong H, Taremi M, Bezjak A, Faria S, Dagnault A, Plourde ME, Bouchard M, Lok BH, Qu XM, Turgeon GA, Rodrigues GB, Yaremko BP, Yu E, Lavoie C, Ashworth A, Gaede S, Sun A. PET-Guided Dose Escalation for Stage III Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer: A Randomized Clinical Trial. *International Journal of Radiation Oncology, Biology, Physics*. Published online October 23, 2026.[^3]

Journal/source: International Journal of Radiation Oncology, Biology, Physics (IJROBP)  
Publication type: Peer-reviewed randomized clinical trial; numerical results not independently verified  
One-line takeaway: A new randomized study directly tests whether FDG-PET-directed escalation to metabolically active tumour subvolumes improves locoregional outcomes over standard chemoradiation.

Evidence tier: Randomized comparative design, potentially high clinical relevance; **outcome-level evidence assessment provisional** because the primary numerical results were inaccessible during this run.

Importance score: 8/10 (provisional)

Key limitations:

- The numerical efficacy and toxicity results could not be independently verified from an accessible primary abstract or manuscript.
- PET-based target definition, treatment adaptation, organ-at-risk dose, and contemporary consolidation immunotherapy can materially affect applicability.
- Prior thoracic dose-escalation experience warns against assuming improved tumour dose distribution produces better survival.

Practical radiation oncology relevance:
This trial deserves priority reading because stage III NSCLC remains a major unmet local-control problem, while indiscriminate escalation has previously worsened outcomes. The key appraisal questions are **the actual locoregional-failure effect size, serious cardiopulmonary and esophageal toxicity, survival, the boost geometry and dose, and how durvalumab-era management was represented**. Until those results are verified, this digest does **not** endorse PET-guided dose escalation as a new standard. The paper's existence, publication date, randomized design, author list, and PubMed identifier were verified; its DOI and numerical endpoints remain unverified here.

## Worth Skimming

### 4. Agentic Automation of BT-RADS Scoring Using an End-to-End System for Standardized Glioma Follow-up Assessment

Citation: Jabal MS, Zhang J, LaBella D, Houk JL, Zhang D, Rudie JD, Mazurowski MA, Magudia K, Calabrese E. Agentic Automation of BT-RADS Scoring Using an End-to-End System for Standardized Glioma Follow-up Assessment. *Radiology: Artificial Intelligence*. Published online October 21, 2026; e260234.[^4]

Journal/source: Radiology: Artificial Intelligence  
Publication type: Peer-reviewed retrospective multimodal AI technical validation study  
One-line takeaway: A multiagent workflow integrating clinical-note extraction, automated tumour segmentation, and BT-RADS rules improved agreement with an expert reference compared with initial clinical assessments.

Evidence tier: Moderate for technical feasibility; low for clinical effectiveness. Retrospective single-centre validation on 492 evaluable follow-up examinations.

Importance score: 7.5/10

Key limitations:

- Single-centre retrospective cohort with no independent external validation.
- Overall accuracy was 76.0%, leaving clinically important misclassification risk; category-specific sensitivity was variable.
- Comparison with original clinical assessments is not the same as demonstrating better patient management, time savings, or safety in prospective use.

Practical radiation oncology relevance:
The system analyzed **509 consecutive examinations**, of which **492** were evaluable. Accuracy versus expert reference was **76.0% (374/492)**, compared with **57.5% (283/492)** for initial assessments, an 18.5-percentage-point difference. It combines contextual facts such as steroid and bevacizumab exposure and radiation timing with imaging-derived volumetrics, which is closer to real clinical decision-making than image-only segmentation. The most plausible near-term role is a **human-reviewed second reader** for standardized glioma follow-up, not automated progression adjudication or treatment adaptation. The work was previously available as an arXiv preprint; the October 21 peer-reviewed version is the publication ranked here, not a new preprint.

### 5. Harmonizing Radiotherapy Data Standards: A Comparative Analysis of OMOP, CDISC, FHIR, NAACCR, and OBO Foundry With an O3-Anchored Mapping Framework

Citation: Author list not independently verified. Harmonizing Radiotherapy Data Standards: A Comparative Analysis of OMOP, CDISC, FHIR, NAACCR, and OBO Foundry With an O3-Anchored Mapping Framework. *International Journal of Radiation Oncology, Biology, Physics*. Published online October 22, 2026.[^5]

Journal/source: International Journal of Radiation Oncology, Biology, Physics (IJROBP)  
Publication type: Peer-reviewed special report / conceptual interoperability framework  
One-line takeaway: A standards-mapping analysis identifies why radiotherapy data are difficult to exchange across clinical, registry, and research systems and proposes a shared mapping framework.

Evidence tier: Technical/conceptual report; not an intervention study or demonstration of improved patient outcomes.

Importance score: 7/10

Key limitations:

- Framework-level comparison does not demonstrate real-world adoption, interoperability performance, or patient benefit.
- Data standards and software implementations change, and vendor-specific export limitations remain.
- The author list, DOI, and detailed proposed mappings could not be independently verified in this run.

Practical radiation oncology relevance:
This is more consequential than its abstract subject suggests. Scalable contour QA, automated chart checks, toxicity models, adaptive planning research, and multi-centre audits all depend on reliable linkage of **structure names, dose distributions, treatment fractions, imaging, and outcomes**. OMOP, CDISC, FHIR, NAACCR, and ontology-based systems were designed for different data purposes; radiation-specific semantic mapping is needed to avoid losing treatment meaning during exchange. Department leaders and medical physicists developing automation or research pipelines should monitor the proposed standards and assess whether their local data exports preserve clinically meaningful dose and treatment metadata.


### 6. A Phase 1 Trial Investigating the Addition of Papaverine to Stereotactic Body Radiotherapy (SBRT) for Hypoxic Radiosensitization of Lung Tumors

Citation: Brownstein J, Williams T, Xu-Welliver M, Sebastian N, Ma SJ, Haglund K, Sim A, Benej M, Papandreou I, Qing K, Wei L, Graves E, Denko N, et al. A Phase 1 Trial Investigating the Addition of Papaverine to Stereotactic Body Radiotherapy (SBRT) for Hypoxic Radiosensitization of Lung Tumors. *International Journal of Radiation Oncology, Biology, Physics*. Published online October 22, 2026.[^6]

Journal/source: International Journal of Radiation Oncology, Biology, Physics (IJROBP)  
Publication type: Peer-reviewed phase I dose-finding and safety trial  
One-line takeaway: Papaverine, a repurposed mitochondrial inhibitor, was combined with lung SBRT in a small early-phase study to explore the feasibility of reducing hypoxia-associated radioresistance.

Evidence tier: Early clinical safety/feasibility evidence; no randomized efficacy evidence.

Importance score: 6.5/10

Key limitations:

- Only 19 patients were enrolled and 18 were evaluable, without a concurrent SBRT-alone control arm.
- Dose-finding and tolerability, not proof of improved tumour control, were the principal purposes of phase I testing.
- Patients with early lung primaries and limited metastases may have heterogeneous baseline prognosis and local-control expectations.

Practical radiation oncology relevance:
Patients received papaverine **0.5, 1, or 2 mg/kg** approximately **30–90 minutes before each SBRT fraction**; the reported SBRT regimen was **50 Gy in four or five fractions**. The evaluated maximum tolerated dose was reported as **2 mg/kg**. The small cohort had encouraging reported local control, but local control after modern lung SBRT is already high and the study cannot establish an added drug effect. This is worth watching for larger controlled trials, especially given the longstanding difficulty of translating hypoxia-targeting strategies into practice. The exact DOI was not independently verified.

## Save for Later

### 7. Escalation of Resting Heart Rate and Tachycardia as a Response to Thoracic Radiotherapy: Implications for Cardiovascular Outcomes After Radiotherapy

Citation: Ajdari A, Bobic M, Ciausu C, Cooper A, Guthier C, Neilan TG, Bortfeld T, Willers H, Jimenez RB, Keane FK. Escalation of Resting Heart Rate and Tachycardia as a Response to Thoracic Radiotherapy: Implications for Cardiovascular Outcomes After Radiotherapy. *International Journal of Radiation Oncology, Biology, Physics*. Published online October 22, 2026.[^7]

Journal/source: International Journal of Radiation Oncology, Biology, Physics (IJROBP)  
Publication type: Peer-reviewed clinical observational biomarker study; detailed methods and effect estimates not independently verified  
One-line takeaway: Resting heart-rate changes during thoracic radiotherapy are investigated as a potentially accessible physiological signal of radiation-associated cardiovascular effects.

Evidence tier: Observational clinical association; insufficient evidence for prospective toxicity prediction or clinical decision support.

Importance score: 6/10

Key limitations:

- Heart rate is nonspecific and is affected by infection, anemia, dehydration, medication, pain, anxiety, and concurrent systemic treatment.
- Causal links between dose to cardiac substructures, tachycardia, and late cardiovascular events require careful confounding control.
- Numerical effect estimates, follow-up duration, and independent validation were not verified in the accessible source material.

Practical radiation oncology relevance:
Routine on-treatment vital signs are attractive for longitudinal toxicity surveillance because they require no new imaging or specialized assay. The important next question is whether a heart-rate trajectory adds independent predictive value beyond baseline cardiovascular risk, delivered cardiac dose, concurrent treatment, and conventional symptoms. This is a hypothesis for prospective validation, not a reason to change thoracic constraints or use tachycardia alone to interrupt treatment.

### 8. Radiation Vasculopathy of the Coronary Arteries: State-of-the-Art Review

Citation: Full author list not independently verified. Radiation Vasculopathy of the Coronary Arteries: State-of-the-Art Review. *Circulation*. 2026;154(16):1627–1645. Published online October 19, 2026.[^8]

Journal/source: Circulation  
Publication type: Peer-reviewed state-of-the-art clinical review  
One-line takeaway: A contemporary cardiovascular review revisits mechanisms, clinical recognition, and management of coronary vascular disease associated with radiation exposure.

Evidence tier: Expert review synthesizing heterogeneous observational, mechanistic, and clinical literature.

Importance score: 6/10

Key limitations:

- A review cannot establish causal thresholds for specific coronary substructures.
- Much of the long-term evidence comes from historical treatment techniques and heterogeneous patient populations.
- The full author list and detailed recommendations were not independently retrieved during this surveillance pass.

Practical radiation oncology relevance:
For breast, lung, mediastinal lymphoma, and other thoracic radiotherapy, late cardiovascular risk should be considered alongside target coverage and competing cancer risks. The review is useful background for understanding why whole-heart mean dose alone may be insufficient and why baseline risk factors, substructure exposure, latency, and cardiovascular prevention matter. It does not replace site-specific modern planning constraints or cardiology evaluation.

## Low Priority

### 9. A Senescence-Driven Fibroblast-Macrophage Feed-Forward Circuit Promotes Radiation-Induced Cutaneous Fibrosis

Citation: Chung et al. A senescence-driven fibroblast-macrophage feed-forward circuit promotes radiation-induced cutaneous fibrosis. *International Journal of Radiation Oncology, Biology, Physics*. Published online October 23, 2026. Complete author list and DOI not independently verified.[^9]

Journal/source: International Journal of Radiation Oncology, Biology, Physics (IJROBP)  
Publication type: Peer-reviewed mechanistic radiobiology study; full experimental design not independently verified  
One-line takeaway: A proposed senescence-associated fibroblast-macrophage signalling loop offers a possible biological explanation for radiation-induced skin fibrosis.

Evidence tier: Mechanistic and hypothesis-generating; not evidence of an effective clinical antifibrotic intervention.

Importance score: 4.5/10

Key limitations:

- The detailed model systems, sample sizes, and effect estimates were not independently available in this surveillance pass.
- Identifying a pathway does not establish that pharmacological targeting improves human fibrosis.
- Translation requires clinically relevant fractionation models, safety evaluation, and prospective human endpoints.

Practical radiation oncology relevance:
Late cutaneous fibrosis is an important survivorship problem, but this paper currently belongs in the translational watchlist. It may inform future radiosensitivity or antifibrotic research; it should not change dose constraints, patient counselling about absolute risk, or treatment of established fibrosis without clinical intervention data.

## Overall Ranking Summary

### Must Read

- 1. **HypoMast-IMNI** — 9.5/10; phase III randomized evidence directly supporting 16-fraction PMRT including internal mammary nodes.
- 2. **ESTRO/ReCOG Lung Reirradiation Consensus** — 9/10; highly practical technical safety guidance, not randomized evidence.
- 3. **PET-Guided Dose Escalation for Stage III NSCLC** — 8/10, provisional; randomized study with outcome data requiring primary-paper verification.

### Worth Skimming

- 4. **Agentic BT-RADS Glioma Follow-up** — 7.5/10; clinically contextual AI validation, but single-centre and not ready for unsupervised deployment.
- 5. **Harmonizing Radiotherapy Data Standards** — 7/10; operationally important interoperability framework, not patient-outcome evidence.
- 6. **Papaverine Plus Lung SBRT** — 6.5/10; phase I safety signal without comparative efficacy.

### Save for Later

- 7. **Resting Heart Rate During Thoracic RT** — 6/10; exploratory physiological toxicity biomarker.
- 8. **Radiation Vasculopathy of the Coronary Arteries** — 6/10; clinically relevant review of late effects.

### Low Priority

- 9. **Senescence-Driven Fibroblast-Macrophage Circuit in Cutaneous Fibrosis** — 4.5/10; mechanistic hypothesis without demonstrated clinical intervention.
