# Weekly Medical News and Clinical Horizon Scan: July 27–August 2, 2026

## Bottom Line This Week

The most clinically important trial was a randomized study of **perioperative dapagliflozin in cardiac surgery**, which reduced acute kidney injury within seven days from 52% to 28%. The effect size is striking, but the primary endpoint was short-term and creatinine-based, with no clear improvement in harder clinical outcomes yet. This is potentially practice-changing, not immediately practice-settling.

The most important negative trial was **TRACK**, in which low-dose rivaroxaban failed to prevent major cardiovascular events in stage 4 or 5 chronic kidney disease and significantly increased major bleeding. This result is directly actionable and argues against extrapolating “dual-pathway” vascular prevention into advanced CKD.

The most important new therapeutic development was the FDA expansion of **lutetium-177 vipivotide tetraxetan (Pluvicto)** to PSMA-positive metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer in combination with an androgen-receptor pathway inhibitor. This moves radioligand therapy substantially earlier in the prostate-cancer course, although it is a US approval and does not automatically establish Canadian availability or reimbursement.

Other high-value developments included a phase III **once-weekly oral islatravir–lenacapavir** regimen that maintained HIV suppression after switching from daily therapy; a culturally tailored Indigenous food intervention associated with fewer heart-failure acute-care encounters; and a negative care-home trial showing that portable HEPA filters did not reduce respiratory infections.

The key safety and public-health update was a WHO statement affirming the favourable benefit–risk profile of properly prescribed **GLP-1 receptor agonists**, while warning against unsupervised use, falsified products, inappropriate prescribing, and inadequate adverse-event monitoring.

Overall, this was a **clinically important week**, driven by several randomized trials with immediate implications for kidney, cardiovascular, infectious-disease, and health-system practice. The strongest evidence was more useful for refining care than for introducing entirely new paradigms.

## Must Know

### 1. Dapagliflozin Substantially Reduces Acute Kidney Injury After Cardiac Surgery

Citation: Dapagliflozin and Acute Kidney Injury Following Cardiac Surgery: A Randomized Clinical Trial.[^1]

Journal/source: JAMA

Publication type: Peer-reviewed randomized placebo-controlled clinical trial

One-line takeaway: Perioperative dapagliflozin reduced acute kidney injury within seven days after cardiac surgery from 52% to 28%.

Evidence tier: Randomized clinical evidence with a large relative and absolute reduction in a prespecified postoperative kidney endpoint.

Evidence maturity: Potentially practice-changing.

Practice impact: Possibly soon.

Canadian relevance:
Dapagliflozin is available in Canada for several diabetes, heart-failure, and kidney indications, but perioperative prevention of cardiac-surgery–associated acute kidney injury is not an established Health Canada indication. Canadian adoption would require confirmation of the dosing protocol, perioperative safety, and benefit on harder outcomes, as well as coordination among cardiac surgery, anesthesia, cardiology, endocrinology, and nephrology teams.

Importance score: 9/10

Key limitations:

* The primary outcome was acute kidney injury within seven days, largely defined by creatinine changes.
* The study did not demonstrate clear improvement in mortality, dialysis, length of stay, or other major patient-centred outcomes.
* The population was predominantly White, which may limit generalizability.

Practical medical relevance:
The relative risk was approximately 0.54, corresponding to a 24-percentage-point absolute reduction. That magnitude is difficult to ignore, but perioperative SGLT2 inhibitor use also raises concerns about volume status and euglycemic ketoacidosis. Practice should not change on the headline alone without reviewing the protocol, exclusions, safety monitoring, and confirmatory evidence.

### 2. Low-Dose Rivaroxaban Fails in Advanced CKD and Increases Major Bleeding

Citation: Badve SV, Perkovic V, Jha V, et al. Low-Dose Rivaroxaban and Cardiovascular Events in Advanced Kidney Disease: The TRACK Randomized Clinical Trial.[^2]

Journal/source: JAMA

Publication type: Peer-reviewed randomized double-blind placebo-controlled clinical trial

One-line takeaway: In stage 4 or 5 CKD, low-dose rivaroxaban did not reduce cardiovascular events and increased major bleeding.

Evidence tier: Large randomized outcomes trial using cardiovascular events and major bleeding.

Evidence maturity: Ready for practice.

Practice impact: Now.

Canadian relevance:
Rivaroxaban is available in Canada, but this trial does not support adding low-dose rivaroxaban for vascular prevention in patients with advanced CKD solely because they are at high cardiovascular risk. The finding is highly applicable to Canadian nephrology, cardiology, internal medicine, and primary care.

Importance score: 9/10

Key limitations:

* The trial was stopped early for lack of efficacy.
* The population had advanced CKD and the findings should not be generalized to patients with preserved kidney function.
* The study addressed vascular prevention, not established indications such as treatment of venous thromboembolism or atrial fibrillation.

Practical medical relevance:
Major cardiovascular events occurred in 22.6% of the rivaroxaban group and 20.7% of the placebo group. Major bleeding occurred in 8.8% versus 6.0%, respectively. The net message is clear: advanced CKD changes the balance between thrombosis and bleeding, and benefits seen in broader atherosclerotic populations cannot be assumed to apply.

### 3. Pluvicto Moves Into PSMA-Positive Metastatic Hormone-Sensitive Prostate Cancer

Citation: US Food and Drug Administration. FDA approves lutetium Lu 177 vipivotide tetraxetan with androgen receptor pathway inhibitor therapy for PSMA-positive metastatic androgen pathway modulation–naïve or –sensitive prostate cancer.[^3]

Journal/source: US Food and Drug Administration

Publication type: Regulatory approval

One-line takeaway: The FDA approved Pluvicto with an androgen-receptor pathway inhibitor for PSMA-positive metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer.

Evidence tier: Regulatory decision supported by clinical-trial evidence submitted to the FDA.

Evidence maturity: Ready for US practice.

Practice impact: Now in the United States; possibly soon elsewhere.

Canadian relevance:
This is a US regulatory decision. Health Canada approval and Canadian public reimbursement for this earlier disease setting could not be verified as of August 2, 2026. Canadian implementation would also depend on PSMA-PET access, nuclear-medicine capacity, radiopharmacy infrastructure, radiation-safety workflows, and provincial funding.

Importance score: 9/10

Key limitations:

* The FDA announcement is not a substitute for full review of the underlying trial publication and absolute outcome data.
* Access may be limited by nuclear-medicine capacity and radioligand supply.
* Earlier treatment increases the number of patients exposed to marrow, renal, salivary, and cumulative radiation risks.

Practical medical relevance:
Radioligand therapy has rapidly moved from late-line metastatic castration-resistant disease toward earlier treatment. This approval may reorganize sequencing among androgen-receptor pathway inhibitors, chemotherapy, radioligand therapy, and metastasis-directed approaches, with major operational implications for oncology systems.

## Important Developments

### 4. Once-Weekly Oral Islatravir–Lenacapavir Maintains HIV Suppression

Citation: Phase 3 Trial of Weekly Oral Islatravir–Lenacapavir for HIV-1 Treatment.[^4]

Journal/source: New England Journal of Medicine

Publication type: Peer-reviewed phase III randomized switch trial

One-line takeaway: Switching virologically suppressed adults from daily bictegravir–emtricitabine–tenofovir alafenamide to once-weekly oral islatravir–lenacapavir maintained viral suppression at 48 weeks.

Evidence tier: Phase III randomized noninferiority evidence.

Evidence maturity: Potentially practice-changing.

Practice impact: Possibly soon.

Canadian relevance:
The combination remains investigational pending regulatory decisions. Canadian relevance will depend on Health Canada review, resistance requirements, drug interactions, cost, adherence patterns, and whether public drug plans fund a premium for weekly rather than daily oral therapy.

Importance score: 8/10

Key limitations:

* This was a switch study in patients who were already virologically suppressed.
* Noninferiority does not establish superiority in adherence, quality of life, resistance, or long-term outcomes.
* Longer follow-up is needed for resistance and safety, particularly given the long pharmacologic tail of lenacapavir.

Practical medical relevance:
A complete once-weekly oral regimen could reduce daily pill burden without requiring injections. Its greatest value may be for patients who prefer oral treatment but struggle with daily dosing, although missing a weekly dose may carry different adherence and resistance consequences than missing a daily dose.

### 5. Culturally Tailored Traditional Meals Reduce Acute-Care Use in Diné Patients With Heart Failure

Citation: Eberly LA, George C, Sandman S, et al. An Indigenous Food Is Medicine Intervention: The MUTTON-HF Randomized Clinical Trial.[^5]

Journal/source: JAMA Internal Medicine

Publication type: Pragmatic open-label randomized clinical trial

One-line takeaway: An eight-week program of medically and culturally tailored traditional meals was associated with fewer hospitalizations and emergency-department visits among Diné adults with heart failure.

Evidence tier: Small pragmatic randomized evidence focused on healthcare utilization.

Evidence maturity: Awaiting replication and implementation.

Practice impact: Possibly soon in adapted programs.

Canadian relevance:
The trial was conducted in the Navajo Nation and should not be directly transplanted into Canadian Indigenous communities. The principle is highly relevant, however: programs should be community-led, culturally grounded, locally sourced, and developed in partnership with First Nations, Inuit, and Métis communities rather than imposed as generic “healthy meal” interventions.

Importance score: 8/10

Key limitations:

* The trial included approximately 206 participants at two Indian Health Service sites.
* It was open-label and relatively short.
* Generalizability depends heavily on local culture, food systems, geography, and healthcare infrastructure.

Practical medical relevance:
This is more than a nutrition trial. It tests whether healthcare systems can address food insecurity, cultural safety, and chronic-disease management together. The signal suggests that appropriately designed social and nutritional interventions can affect hospital use, not merely biomarkers.

### 6. Portable HEPA Filters Do Not Reduce Respiratory Infections in Care Homes

Citation: Hay AD, Brierley RCM, Turner N, et al. High-Efficiency Particulate Air Filters to Prevent Winter Respiratory Infections in Care Homes: The AFRI-c Cluster Randomized Clinical Trial.[^6]

Journal/source: JAMA Internal Medicine

Publication type: Peer-reviewed cluster randomized clinical trial

One-line takeaway: HEPA filters placed in communal spaces and bedrooms did not reduce respiratory-infection episodes among care-home residents.

Evidence tier: Pragmatic cluster randomized evidence.

Evidence maturity: Ready to inform implementation.

Practice impact: Now.

Canadian relevance:
The result is relevant to Canadian long-term-care facilities considering portable air cleaners as an infection-prevention intervention. It does not mean ventilation and air quality are unimportant, but it argues against relying on portable HEPA units alone instead of vaccination, sick-leave policies, isolation, masking during outbreaks, hand hygiene, and broader ventilation improvements.

Importance score: 7/10

Key limitations:

* The effectiveness of filters depends on room size, air changes, placement, maintenance, and adherence.
* Infection outcomes may have been influenced by changing viral epidemiology and other infection-control practices.
* Results from care homes may not apply to hospitals, schools, private homes, or purpose-built ventilation systems.

Practical medical relevance:
The trial found no reduction in respiratory infections, antibiotic prescriptions, hospitalizations, or staff sickness. This is useful negative evidence for facilities deciding where to spend limited infection-control funds.

### 7. The First FDA-Licensed Freeze-Dried Plasma Product Could Expand Emergency Access

Citation: US Food and Drug Administration. FDA licenses Ezplaz, the first freeze-dried plasma product in the United States.[^7]

Journal/source: US Food and Drug Administration

Publication type: Regulatory approval of a blood product

One-line takeaway: Freeze-dried plasma can be stored and rapidly reconstituted where conventional thawed plasma is unavailable.

Evidence tier: Regulatory review supported by manufacturing, hemostatic, and clinical evidence.

Evidence maturity: Ready for selected US use.

Practice impact: Possibly soon.

Canadian relevance:
A corresponding Health Canada authorization could not be verified as of August 2, 2026. The product could be particularly relevant to Canadian military medicine, air ambulance, remote and rural care, trauma systems, and facilities without immediate access to thawed plasma, but implementation would require Canadian Blood Services or Héma-Québec coordination and national transfusion-policy review.

Importance score: 7/10

Key limitations:

* It is intended for situations in which other plasma products are unavailable, not as a universal replacement.
* Supply, cost, compatibility, reconstitution time, and transfusion reactions remain practical considerations.
* Evidence for patient-centred superiority over conventional plasma strategies may be limited.

Practical medical relevance:
The logistical advantage is the central innovation. A shelf-stable or rapidly deployable plasma product could shorten time to balanced resuscitation in remote trauma, prehospital care, austere environments, and mass-casualty settings.

## Safety, Regulation, and Public Health

### 8. WHO Reaffirms GLP-1 Benefits but Warns About Misuse and Product Quality

Citation: World Health Organization. Statement on the safe and appropriate use of GLP-1 receptor agonists.[^8]

Journal/source: World Health Organization advisory committees on medicines and vaccine safety

Publication type: International medication-safety statement

One-line takeaway: WHO affirmed that GLP-1 receptor agonists have a favourable benefit–risk profile when appropriately prescribed, while emphasizing qualified supervision, authentic products, and adverse-event monitoring.

Evidence tier: Expert regulatory and pharmacovigilance assessment.

Evidence maturity: Ready for practice.

Practice impact: Now.

Canadian relevance:
Semaglutide, liraglutide, tirzepatide, and related agents have product-specific Health Canada indications. Canadian clinicians should prescribe only authorized products through regulated supply chains and should not assume that compounded, imported, or online products are equivalent. Coverage and obesity-treatment access remain uneven across provinces and private plans.

Importance score: 8/10

Key limitations:

* The statement is a safety and appropriate-use document, not a new randomized trial.
* It does not resolve product-specific differences in efficacy, safety, or access.
* Rare adverse effects require ongoing pharmacovigilance and cannot be fully excluded by preapproval trials.

Practical medical relevance:
The statement is timely because global demand has increased off-label use, counterfeit products, unsupervised dose escalation, and prescribing outside comprehensive chronic-disease care. Patients should be counselled about gastrointestinal effects, dehydration, gallbladder disease, pancreatitis symptoms, peri-procedural considerations, and avoiding unregulated products.

### 9. FDA Broadens Arexvy’s Indication to At-Risk Adults Aged 18 to 59 Years

Citation: US Food and Drug Administration. Arexvy prescribing information and approval materials.[^9]

Journal/source: US Food and Drug Administration

Publication type: Regulatory indication expansion

One-line takeaway: The US indication now includes adults aged 18 to 59 years who are at increased risk for RSV lower respiratory tract disease.

Evidence tier: Regulatory decision supported primarily by immunogenicity and safety bridging data in younger high-risk adults.

Evidence maturity: Ready for US practice.

Practice impact: Now in the United States.

Canadian relevance:
Canadian RSV vaccine recommendations and product authorizations are separate from the FDA decision. Canadian clinicians should follow NACI, Health Canada, and provincial guidance rather than assuming that all adults aged 18 to 59 years with risk factors are currently eligible or publicly funded.

Importance score: 7/10

Key limitations:

* The expansion relies substantially on immune-response bridging rather than a large clinical-endpoint efficacy trial in every younger risk group.
* “Increased risk” encompasses heterogeneous conditions with varying absolute benefit.
* Public reimbursement may be more restrictive than regulatory authorization.

Practical medical relevance:
This decision extends RSV vaccination beyond older adults and may affect patients with chronic lung or cardiovascular disease, immunocompromise, and other risk factors. The central clinical task is identifying who has enough absolute risk to justify vaccination.

## On the Horizon

These developments are **not ready for routine clinical practice**.

### 10. A Kidney-Risk Model Using Longitudinal Laboratory Trajectories Shows Incremental Improvement

Citation: Sanchez M, Diao JA, Cummings J, et al. Laboratory Trajectories Improve Kidney Failure Risk Estimation.[^10]

Journal/source: arXiv preprint

Publication type: Preprint; retrospective model-development and validation study

One-line takeaway: Incorporating repeated laboratory trajectories modestly improved kidney-failure risk prediction compared with single latest-value models.

Evidence tier: Large retrospective modelling evidence without prospective clinical-impact validation.

Evidence maturity: Early clinical evidence.

Practice impact: Long-term.

Canadian relevance:
The approach could eventually improve referral timing and dialysis planning, but it has not been prospectively validated in Canadian health systems or shown to improve patient outcomes. Deployment would require local calibration, fairness assessment, workflow integration, and evaluation of false-positive referrals.

Importance score: 6/10

Key limitations:

* The work is a preprint and has not yet undergone peer review.
* Improvements in discrimination were modest.
* Better prediction does not prove better care, and no prospective intervention trial was performed.

Practical medical relevance:
Longitudinal EHR data contain information that static risk equations ignore. The credible next step is not immediate deployment but prospective testing of whether trajectory-aware risk estimates improve nephrology referral, transplant preparation, modality education, and patient outcomes.

## Hype Check

### 11. Ziltivekimab Lowers Inflammatory Biomarkers but Fails to Prevent Cardiovascular Events

Citation: Novo Nordisk. Headline results from the ZEUS phase III cardiovascular outcomes trial of ziltivekimab.[^11]

Journal/source: Company press release with independent medical reporting

Publication type: Phase III company announcement; full peer-reviewed results not yet available

One-line takeaway: Despite strong IL-6 pathway and C-reactive-protein effects, ziltivekimab did not reduce major adverse cardiovascular events and was associated with more serious infections.

Evidence tier: Large phase III outcome-trial topline result, but available only through a press release.

Evidence maturity: Awaiting full publication.

Practice impact: Unlikely to change practice except by halting or redirecting development.

Canadian relevance:
Ziltivekimab is investigational and not approved in Canada. The result has no immediate prescribing implication but is scientifically important for anti-inflammatory cardiovascular drug development.

Importance score: 7/10

Key limitations:

* Full event rates, hazard ratios, subgroup findings, and detailed safety data are not yet publicly available.
* Press-release reporting limits independent interpretation.
* Failure of one IL-6–targeted agent does not exclude all inflammation-targeted cardiovascular strategies.

Practical medical relevance:
The trial enrolled more than 6,300 patients with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, CKD, and systemic inflammation. It is a classic surrogate-endpoint caution: dramatic biomarker suppression did not translate into fewer heart attacks, strokes, or cardiovascular deaths.

## Weekly Takeaways

**Most likely to change practice:** The dapagliflozin cardiac-surgery trial, if the protocol and safety findings are confirmed and the kidney benefit translates into harder outcomes.

**Most important new therapeutic development:** Pluvicto moving into PSMA-positive metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer.

**Most important safety or public-health update:** WHO’s GLP-1 statement emphasizing appropriate prescribing, regulated products, and pharmacovigilance amid rapidly expanding use.

**Most interesting emerging technology:** Freeze-dried plasma for emergency, prehospital, remote, and austere-care settings.

**Most overhyped story:** IL-6 biomarker suppression with ziltivekimab; the phase III outcomes trial shows that target engagement is not equivalent to cardiovascular benefit.

**One development physicians may be underestimating:** The negative HEPA-filter trial in care homes, because it can prevent facilities from investing heavily in a stand-alone intervention without demonstrated infection benefit.

**What patients may ask about this week:** Whether SGLT2 inhibitors should be taken around surgery, whether weekly HIV pills are available, whether Pluvicto is now a first-line prostate-cancer treatment, who should receive an RSV vaccine, and whether online or compounded GLP-1 products are safe.
