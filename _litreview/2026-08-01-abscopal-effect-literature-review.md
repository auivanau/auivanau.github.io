# The Abscopal Effect and Systemic Immune Effects of Radiation Therapy: Biology, Clinical Evidence, and Future Directions

**Audience:** radiation oncology residents and clinically oriented oncology readers  
**Review type:** critical narrative review  
**Search cutoff:** August 1, 2026  
**Estimated reading time:** approximately 20 minutes

## Executive summary

The **abscopal effect** is the regression of a tumor lesion outside the irradiated field after localized radiation therapy. The modern biologic interpretation is predominantly immune-mediated: radiation can release tumor antigens and danger signals, activate innate immune sensing pathways, support dendritic-cell cross-presentation, and generate tumor-specific T cells capable of attacking distant metastases. The effect is biologically credible and well supported in animal models, but clinically it remains uncommon, inconsistently defined, and difficult to separate from concurrent systemic therapy.

A central distinction runs through this review. The **strict abscopal effect** means objective regression of a genuinely nonirradiated lesion after local radiation, ideally documented lesion-by-lesion and confirmed on follow-up. **Radiation-immunotherapy synergy** is broader: radiation may improve systemic disease control, progression-free survival, overall survival, or immune activation without necessarily producing a visible response in an untreated lesion. The two concepts overlap but are not interchangeable.

The strongest mechanistic evidence comes from preclinical models showing that radiation-induced systemic responses often require CD8-positive T cells, dendritic-cell cross-presentation, type I interferon signaling, and in some settings cGAS-STING activation. Radiation can also increase immune checkpoints and suppressive pathways, including PD-L1, TGF-beta, myeloid suppression, lymphocyte depletion, marrow exposure, and damage to tumor-draining lymph nodes. Therefore, radiation is not inherently immunostimulatory; its net immune effect depends on dose, fractionation, target, field design, tumor biology, host immune competence, and the systemic therapy context.

The clinical evidence is mixed. Case reports and proof-of-principle studies demonstrate that striking distant responses can occur. A patient-level pooled analysis of two randomized NSCLC trials suggested improved out-of-field response and survival when radiation was added to pembrolizumab. However, multiple prospective and randomized studies have been neutral or negative, including CHEERS in advanced solid tumors, NIVES in metastatic renal-cell carcinoma, melanoma SBRT-nivolumab data, and the NSCLC trial of durvalumab/tremelimumab with or without radiotherapy after prior PD-(L)1 therapy. These trials show that simply irradiating one or a few lesions during checkpoint blockade is not enough to reliably produce clinically meaningful systemic benefit.

**Clinical bottom line:** outside a clinical trial, radiation should not be prescribed solely to create an abscopal response. It is reasonable to consider immune interactions when radiation is indicated for palliation, oligoprogression, local control, or consolidation, but patients should not be promised distant shrinkage. The key research question is no longer whether abscopal responses can happen. They can. The key question is whether radiation can be used reproducibly, safely, and predictably to improve systemic disease control beyond what immunotherapy would have achieved alone.

## 1. Definition and terminology

The term **abscopal** is usually traced to R. H. Mole’s 1953 discussion of whole-body irradiation biology, where it was used to describe effects occurring “away from” the irradiated volume.[1] In oncology, the term now most commonly means regression of nonirradiated tumor lesions after localized radiation therapy.

A strict clinical definition should include three elements:

1. **A local treatment event**: one or more lesions receive radiation.
2. **A distant measurable lesion**: at least one tumor site lies outside the therapeutic and meaningful incidental-dose region.
3. **Objective regression after radiation**: the distant lesion shrinks by a predefined criterion, ideally confirmed on subsequent imaging.

This seems simple, but clinical interpretation is difficult. Many reported abscopal responses occurred while patients were also receiving immunotherapy, targeted therapy, chemotherapy, steroids, antibiotics, or changing systemic treatment. If pembrolizumab was started two weeks before radiation and liver metastases shrink eight weeks later, the distant response could reflect pembrolizumab alone, a delayed checkpoint response, a radiation-amplified immune response, or a combination.

For that reason, the term **out-of-field response** is often more neutral than abscopal effect in trials. It describes the observation—regression of nonirradiated lesions—without overclaiming causality. A randomized control arm is usually needed to determine whether radiation truly increased the probability of systemic response.

## 2. Suggested criteria for measuring an abscopal response

Because definitions vary across reports, a rigorous trial-level definition should specify:

- which lesions are irradiated;
- which lesions are evaluable as nonirradiated targets;
- the minimum distance or dosimetric exclusion criterion for “nonirradiated,” such as exclusion from a defined low-dose isodose region;
- whether response is assessed by RECIST 1.1, iRECIST, volumetry, metabolic imaging, or another method;
- whether the endpoint is patient-level or lesion-level;
- whether regression must be confirmed on repeat imaging;
- the time window after radiation;
- whether concurrent systemic therapy is allowed, fixed, or changed; and
- whether pseudoprogression or immune-related response patterns are handled prospectively.[2,3]

A practical working definition for a clinical radiation-immunotherapy study could be:

> An abscopal response is a complete or partial response by a predefined response criterion in at least one measurable nonirradiated lesion that received no clinically meaningful radiation dose, occurring after localized radiation and confirmed on follow-up imaging, with systemic treatment controlled or clearly documented.

This definition does not prove causation by itself. It merely standardizes the event. Causality requires a comparison group or a strong longitudinal argument that the lesion was previously progressing and that no competing systemic intervention explains the response.

## 3. Causality: did radiation actually cause the distant response?

The central clinical challenge is not documenting a shrinking distant lesion; it is determining **why** it shrank.

A distant response after radiation during immunotherapy might represent ordinary systemic drug activity. It might also represent a delayed immunotherapy response that would have occurred without radiation. Some tumors have mixed responses, where one lesion grows while another shrinks because of clonal heterogeneity. In immune checkpoint therapy, apparent progression may sometimes precede response, which is why immune response criteria were developed.[4]

A convincing abscopal trial should therefore include baseline imaging showing that nonirradiated lesions were measurable and preferably active, prospective designation of nonirradiated target lesions, lesion-level dosimetry, standardized imaging timing, response confirmation, and a control arm receiving the same systemic therapy without radiation. Without those safeguards, the phrase “abscopal effect” should be used cautiously.

## 4. Biological mechanism: how local radiation can become systemic immunity

Radiation damages tumor DNA and can cause multiple forms of tumor-cell death. Some forms are immunologically silent; others are immunogenic. Immunogenic cell death exposes or releases danger-associated molecular patterns such as calreticulin, ATP, HMGB1, and tumor-derived nucleic acids. These signals can recruit and activate antigen-presenting cells.[5]

Radiation can also increase antigen presentation. In preclinical models, irradiated cancer cells can show increased MHC class I expression and a broader peptide repertoire, making surviving tumor cells more visible to cytotoxic T cells.[6]

A major pathway is **cytosolic DNA sensing**. Radiation can generate micronuclei and double-stranded DNA in the cytosol. cGAS detects cytosolic DNA and generates cGAMP, which activates STING. STING signaling induces type I interferons and inflammatory chemokines. In immunogenic tumor models, STING-dependent type I interferon signaling in dendritic cells is required for radiation-induced adaptive immune responses.[7]

Dendritic cells then take up tumor antigens and cross-present them to T cells, usually in tumor-draining lymph nodes. CD8-positive T cells expand, enter the circulation, traffic to distant metastases, recognize shared tumor antigens, and kill tumor cells. In this sense, radiation can behave like an **in situ vaccine**: the irradiated tumor provides the antigen source, while radiation provides part of the danger signal and inflammatory context.

This model is elegant, but every step can fail. The irradiated lesion may lack relevant antigens. Dendritic-cell recruitment may be poor. The tumor-draining lymph node may be damaged by radiation. T cells may be depleted, exhausted, excluded, or suppressed. Distant metastases may have lost the target antigen, reside in an immunosuppressive organ, or be too bulky for immune control.

## 5. Radiation’s competing immune effects

Radiation has both immune-stimulatory and immune-suppressive effects.

Potentially favorable immune effects include antigen release, immunogenic cell death, MHC-I upregulation, chemokine induction, cGAS-STING activation, type I interferon signaling, dendritic-cell activation, and T-cell trafficking.[5-8]

Potentially unfavorable effects include direct lymphocyte killing, depletion of circulating blood lymphocytes during large-field treatment, marrow irradiation, damage to tumor-draining lymph nodes, TGF-beta activation, PD-L1 upregulation, recruitment or persistence of suppressive myeloid cells, regulatory T-cell effects, adenosine signaling, and tumor hypoxia. Deng and colleagues showed that radiation can increase PD-L1 expression in the tumor microenvironment, creating a rational basis for PD-L1 blockade but also illustrating that radiation-induced inflammation may simultaneously trigger adaptive immune resistance.[8]

TGF-beta is another important suppressive pathway. In poorly immunogenic models, neutralizing TGF-beta during radiation enabled CD8 T-cell responses against multiple endogenous tumor antigens and produced regression of nonirradiated disease.[9]

This bidirectionality matters clinically. Radiation is not automatically an immune accelerator. Depending on how and where it is delivered, radiation may either help generate systemic immunity or damage the immune machinery required for that response.

## 6. Foundational preclinical evidence

Demaria and colleagues demonstrated in a murine mammary carcinoma model that local radiation could inhibit growth of a distant untreated tumor and that this distant effect was at least partly immune mediated. T-cell-deficient mice did not show the same distant inhibition, establishing a key role for T cells.[10]

Lee and colleagues later showed that ablative radiation increased T-cell priming in draining lymphoid tissues and that tumor reduction depended substantially on CD8-positive T cells.[11] This study supported a major shift in thinking: radiation can produce tumor control not only through direct DNA damage but also through host immunity.

Dewan and colleagues compared different fractionation schedules in bilateral tumor models treated with anti-CTLA-4. Radiation alone delayed growth of the irradiated tumor but was insufficient to control the distant tumor. The combination of radiation and anti-CTLA-4 produced abscopal effects with fractionated regimens, particularly 8 Gy × 3 and 6 Gy × 5, but not with a single 20 Gy fraction in those models.[12] This study is why 8 Gy × 3 became a commonly cited “immunogenic” schedule. However, it should be remembered as a preclinical observation in specific models, not a validated universal clinical dose.

Deng and colleagues showed that STING-dependent cytosolic DNA sensing in dendritic cells promotes radiation-induced type I interferon-dependent antitumor immunity.[7] A separate study from Deng and colleagues showed that irradiation and anti-PD-L1 treatment synergistically promoted antitumor immunity in mice, that radiation increased PD-L1 expression, and that CD8-positive T cells were essential for the therapeutic effect.[8]

Vanpouille-Box and colleagues identified the DNA exonuclease TREX1 as an upstream regulator of radiation-driven antitumor immunity. In several cancer cell lines, radiation doses above approximately 12-18 Gy induced TREX1, which degraded cytosolic DNA and attenuated cGAS-STING-driven interferon-beta production. Repeated doses below the TREX1-inducing threshold amplified interferon-beta production and supported Batf3-dependent dendritic-cell activation, CD8 T-cell priming, and systemic tumor rejection with checkpoint blockade.[13]

These foundational studies support a coherent model: radiation can initiate antitumor immunity, but checkpoint blockade or other immune modulation is often needed to make the response clinically meaningful.

## 7. Human proof-of-concept evidence

For decades, clinical abscopal effects were mostly case reports. Case reports are useful because they show biological possibility, but they are highly vulnerable to publication bias. Dramatic responses are written up; negative attempts are not.

The best-known modern case is the Postow melanoma report. A patient with metastatic melanoma receiving ipilimumab had a growing paraspinal mass treated with radiation; subsequently, nonirradiated lesions regressed. The report included immune correlates, including changes in antibodies and responses to NY-ESO-1, supporting the idea that radiation and CTLA-4 blockade may have amplified systemic antitumor immunity.[14]

Golden and colleagues conducted an important proof-of-principle trial combining local radiation with GM-CSF in patients with metastatic solid tumors. The rationale was that GM-CSF might support dendritic-cell maturation and antigen presentation. Among 41 enrolled patients, objective abscopal responses occurred in approximately 27%, including patients with breast cancer, NSCLC, and thymic cancer.[15] This was a major prospective signal, but it was single-arm, heterogeneous, and did not include a randomized comparator.

Systematic reviews and patient-level summaries of reported abscopal cases show that the phenomenon has been described across many tumor types, especially immunogenic cancers such as melanoma, NSCLC, renal-cell carcinoma, lymphoma, and hepatobiliary cancers.[16,17] However, case-based datasets cannot estimate true incidence because reporting is selective.

## 8. Prospective and randomized clinical evidence

### 8.1 NSCLC: the most encouraging randomized signal

The PEMBRO-RT trial randomized patients with metastatic NSCLC to pembrolizumab alone or SBRT to one lesion followed by pembrolizumab. SBRT was 24 Gy in three fractions. The primary endpoint was improvement in overall response rate at 12 weeks. The observed response rate was 18% with pembrolizumab alone and 36% with SBRT plus pembrolizumab, but this did not meet the study’s prespecified statistical threshold. Median PFS was 1.9 months versus 6.6 months, and median OS was 7.6 months versus 15.9 months, numerically favoring radiation but not statistically definitive.[18]

A separate MD Anderson randomized phase I/II trial studied pembrolizumab with or without concurrent radiation in metastatic NSCLC. Patients had lung or liver lesions amenable to radiation and another noncontiguous measurable lesion. Radiation was delivered as either SBRT, commonly 50 Gy in 4 fractions, or hypofractionated radiation such as 45 Gy in 15 fractions depending on lesion suitability. The trial was designed to assess safety and systemic response, but by itself did not establish a definitive systemic benefit from adding radiation.[19]

A patient-level pooled analysis combined PEMBRO-RT and the MD Anderson trial. In 148 patients, best out-of-field response was 19.7% with pembrolizumab alone versus 41.7% with pembrolizumab plus radiation. Median PFS was 4.4 versus 9.0 months, and median OS was 8.7 versus 19.2 months, favoring the radiation arms.[20] This remains one of the strongest clinical signals suggesting that radiation may enhance systemic response to pembrolizumab in metastatic NSCLC.

Important caveats remain. The pooled analysis was not a phase III trial. The component studies used different radiation schedules and timing. Sample sizes were small. The result is clinically intriguing but not practice-changing proof that SBRT should routinely be added solely to create an abscopal effect.

A 2025 multiomic analysis of biospecimens from the PEMBRO-RT program reported that pembrolizumab plus radiation could induce systemic antitumor immune responses, including in immunologically cold NSCLC. This strengthens the translational rationale but still does not replace the need for randomized clinical validation.[21]

### 8.2 Negative and neutral evidence: why enthusiasm must be tempered

The CHEERS randomized phase II trial tested immune checkpoint inhibitors with or without stereotactic radiotherapy to a limited number of metastases in advanced solid tumors. Ninety-six patients were randomized. Median PFS was 2.8 months in the control arm and 4.4 months in the SBRT arm, not a statistically significant improvement. Median OS and objective response rate also did not significantly improve. The study showed that adding SBRT was feasible and locally effective, but did not produce a clinically meaningful systemic benefit in that heterogeneous population.[22]

The NIVES trial combined nivolumab with SBRT in pretreated metastatic renal-cell carcinoma. SBRT was given as 10 Gy × 3 to one lesion shortly after nivolumab initiation. The objective response rate was 17%, below the prespecified target, and the investigators concluded that the study did not provide sufficient evidence that SBRT added systemic benefit to nivolumab in that setting.[23]

A phase II melanoma study combined nivolumab with SBRT, using 24 Gy in three fractions to the largest lesion. The nonirradiated-lesion response rate was 45%, but this was not clearly better than expected nivolumab activity. The authors concluded that the trial did not demonstrate a substantial additional abscopal response from adding SBRT.[24]

The CA184-043 phase III prostate-cancer trial randomized 799 men with docetaxel-pretreated metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer to ipilimumab or placebo after 8 Gy radiation to a bone metastasis. Median OS was 11.2 months with ipilimumab versus 10.0 months with placebo, with a P value of 0.053 in the primary analysis. Toxicity was greater with ipilimumab. Because both arms received radiation, the trial does not isolate radiation’s abscopal effect; it does show that post-radiation CTLA-4 blockade did not clearly improve OS in the primary analysis for this population.[25]

Schoenfeld and colleagues randomized patients with metastatic NSCLC resistant to prior PD-(L)1 therapy to durvalumab plus tremelimumab alone or with low-dose or hypofractionated radiation. Adding radiation did not improve response to dual checkpoint blockade in that PD-(L)1-refractory setting.[26]

Recent retrospective and real-world studies continue to find occasional abscopal responses, especially in melanoma and ICI-refractory populations, but these are highly selected. The ARTIC melanoma study reported abscopal responses in selected patients with malignant melanoma progressing on immune checkpoint inhibitors and receiving radiation, with associations between hypofractionation and benefit.[27] A 2026 large multicentre cohort of ICI-refractory metastatic disease required nonirradiated lesions outside the 10% isodose and serial imaging, reflecting the field’s movement toward more rigorous definitions.[28]

## 9. Why clinical studies disagree

The clinical trial literature is inconsistent because the biology is complex and the trials are heterogeneous.

**Tumor histology matters.** Melanoma, NSCLC, renal-cell carcinoma, lymphoma, and hepatobiliary tumors are overrepresented among published abscopal cases. These tumors are often more immunogenic than prostate, pancreatic, or microsatellite-stable colorectal cancers, though there are exceptions.[16,17]

**Baseline immunotherapy sensitivity matters.** Radiation may be more likely to help when an immune response is almost present but insufficient. In a completely immune-excluded or severely exhausted tumor, radiation alone may not provide the missing immune machinery.

**The systemic therapy context matters.** Anti-CTLA-4, anti-PD-1, anti-PD-L1, TGF-beta blockade, STING agonists, dendritic-cell strategies, and vaccines affect different steps in the immune cycle. “Radiation plus immunotherapy” is not one biological intervention.

**Dose and fractionation matter, but not in a simple way.** Moderate hypofractionation may support antigen release and innate sensing, while very high single doses may increase TREX1 in some models. Higher BED may improve local control and tumor debulking. Low-dose radiation may remodel stroma or myeloid cells. These effects may conflict.

**Target selection matters.** Irradiating a bulky viable lesion could provide more antigen, but large fields may also irradiate more blood and immune cells. Irradiating bone may affect marrow. Irradiating tumor-draining nodes may impair T-cell priming. Irradiating liver metastases may interact with a tolerogenic organ microenvironment.

**Disease burden matters.** A small number of metastases may be more amenable to systemic immune control than widespread bulky disease. In extensive disease, even a real immune response may be too weak to cause measurable regression.

**Endpoint selection matters.** Out-of-field response, abscopal response, overall response rate, disease control, PFS, OS, and immune correlative endpoints answer different questions. A trial can improve local control without improving PFS. It can improve immune activation without producing RECIST responses. It can show lesion-level out-of-field shrinkage without improving survival.

## 10. Dose, fractionation, target selection, and timing

No radiation regimen is clinically validated as the optimal abscopal schedule.

The 8 Gy × 3 schedule is prominent because of Dewan’s preclinical work and because PEMBRO-RT used 24 Gy in three fractions.[12,18] It is a reasonable experimental schedule, but it should not be treated as a magic immunogenic dose. The TREX1 work suggests that very high single doses can degrade cytosolic DNA and blunt interferon signaling in some cell lines, but this does not mean that all doses above 12-18 Gy are clinically immunologically inferior.[13]

High-dose ablative radiation may produce better local control, stronger debulking, and more tumor-antigen release. Low-dose radiation may alter macrophages, stroma, and vasculature. Multisite radiation may reduce tumor burden and broaden antigen exposure, but it can obscure whether later systemic control is truly abscopal rather than direct treatment of more disease.

Target selection should ideally consider viable tumor burden, antigenic relevance, organ microenvironment, marrow exposure, lymph-node drainage, normal-tissue constraints, and the availability of nonirradiated measurable lesions for response assessment.

Timing is also unsettled. Radiation before immunotherapy may release antigen before checkpoint blockade. Concurrent treatment may block adaptive resistance during immune priming. Radiation after checkpoint priming may exploit an already activated T-cell pool. Preclinical sequence studies indicate that the order of PD-1 blockade and radiation can influence CD8 T-cell death and systemic immunity, but clinical schedules remain variable.[29]

Lymphoid-organ dosimetry is an emerging consideration. Tumor-draining lymph nodes can be necessary for priming, and elective nodal irradiation can impair antitumor immunity in preclinical models.[30] That does not justify omitting clinically indicated nodal RT in curative practice. It does suggest that metastatic radioimmunotherapy trials should measure and intentionally design around dose to lymph nodes, marrow, spleen, and circulating blood when feasible.

## 11. Biomarkers and patient selection

No biomarker currently selects patients for abscopal-directed radiation in routine care.

Potential markers include baseline absolute lymphocyte count, PD-L1 expression, tumor mutational burden, neoantigen load, interferon signatures, cGAS-STING competence, Wnt-beta-catenin immune exclusion, T-cell receptor clonality, circulating tumor DNA, and serial immune-cell phenotyping.

The 2025 Nature Cancer multiomic study is important because it examined serial tissue and blood biospecimens from metastatic NSCLC patients treated with pembrolizumab with or without radiation. It supports the concept that radiation can generate systemic immunologic changes and may sensitize some immunologically cold tumors.[21] However, such biomarkers remain exploratory and should be interpreted as tools for trial enrichment, not clinical decision rules.

ctDNA is particularly attractive because it can capture total disease burden and may detect systemic treatment effects earlier than imaging. T-cell receptor sequencing may identify clonal expansion after radiation. Paired biopsies of irradiated and nonirradiated lesions can distinguish local inflammatory changes from systemic immune remodeling.

## 12. Why abscopal responses remain uncommon

A successful abscopal response requires a long chain of events:

1. the irradiated lesion must contain relevant antigens;
2. radiation must release those antigens in an immunogenic context;
3. innate sensing and interferon signaling must activate;
4. dendritic cells must cross-present antigen effectively;
5. tumor-specific T-cell clones must exist;
6. those clones must expand rather than become exhausted;
7. T cells must survive treatment-related lymphotoxicity;
8. T cells must traffic to distant tumors;
9. distant lesions must express the same antigens;
10. the distant microenvironment must permit infiltration and killing;
11. tumor burden must be low enough for immune control; and
12. systemic therapy must support rather than blunt these events.

Failure at any step prevents a measurable abscopal response.

## 13. Safety and practical radiation-oncology implications

Most studies suggest that focal radiation can often be combined with PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors safely, but toxicity is site-specific. Thoracic radiation raises concern for pneumonitis, especially with checkpoint blockade. Bowel radiation overlaps with immune-mediated colitis risk. Liver radiation can complicate immune-mediated hepatitis or hepatic reserve. Brain SRS combined with immunotherapy may increase edema or radionecrosis concerns, although the absolute risk depends on dose, volume, lesion size, prior therapy, and systemic agent.

CTLA-4 combinations generally carry more immune-related toxicity than PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy. CA184-043 illustrated that immune toxicity can be clinically substantial in a heavily pretreated population.[25]

For routine practice:

- prescribe radiation for a standard indication: pain, bleeding, neurologic compromise, local control, oligoprogression, consolidation, or prevention of morbidity;
- coordinate timing with medical oncology when the patient is receiving immunotherapy;
- minimize unnecessary marrow and lymphoid exposure when it does not compromise the oncologic goal;
- avoid promising distant response;
- document whether nonirradiated lesions are measurable if future response interpretation matters;
- consider trial enrollment when the explicit goal is systemic immune priming.

## 14. Emerging strategies

The field is moving beyond “one lesion plus one checkpoint inhibitor.” Emerging approaches include multisite radiation, high-dose plus low-dose “RadScopal” strategies, STING agonists, TLR agonists, CD40 agonists, intratumoral checkpoint blockade, dendritic-cell injection, oncolytic viruses, personalized vaccines, TGF-beta blockade, adenosine-pathway inhibition, myeloid-targeted agents, and newer checkpoint combinations such as LAG-3 or TIGIT strategies.

A small 2024 randomized phase II study tested SBRT with intratumoral dendritic cells and checkpoint antibodies in patients with oligoprogression on anti-PD-1 therapy. It was safe but too small to establish benefit, illustrating both the creativity and the fragility of current translational approaches.[31]

Future trial designs will also increasingly account for immune-related radiation geometry: dose to circulating blood, marrow, spleen, draining lymph nodes, and nonirradiated measurable lesions.

## 15. What future trials must demonstrate

An informative future abscopal trial should include:

- a histology-specific population;
- a randomized control arm;
- fixed systemic therapy timing;
- predefined irradiated targets and nonirradiated measurable lesions;
- lesion-level dosimetry, including incidental dose thresholds;
- standardized response criteria and response confirmation;
- serial blood immune assays;
- ctDNA;
- tumor biopsies when feasible;
- lymphocyte and lymphoid-organ dosimetry;
- explicit separation of local-control benefit from systemic immune benefit;
- adequate power for clinically meaningful endpoints.

The most useful endpoint may not be the romanticized abscopal response itself. It may be systemic disease control, PFS, OS, or durable treatment-free interval, provided the study design can show that radiation added something beyond local ablation.

## 16. Clinical bottom line

The abscopal effect is real but rare. Radiation can modulate systemic antitumor immunity, but clinical reproducibility remains limited. The best current view is:

> The strict abscopal effect is a visible manifestation of a broader phenomenon: radiation-induced modulation of antitumor immunity.

For patients today, radiation should be used for established local or consolidative indications. For research, the goal is to determine how to convert local tumor irradiation into reproducible systemic immune control without increasing toxicity or compromising standard oncologic treatment.
